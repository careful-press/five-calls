My parent got the “grandchild in trouble” call: what to do now

A frightened voice, an arrest or an accident, an urgent need for money, and a plea not to tell anyone. Here's what to do in the first hour, and how to make sure the next call fails.

The short answer

Call the grandchild, or the relative who was supposedly in trouble, on a number you already have. Once you've confirmed they're safe, act on the money straight away: if cash is due to be collected or has just been handed over, call the police, then call your parent's bank. Then report it. Don't wait to see whether the caller rings back. They usually do, and usually with a bigger demand.

How this scam usually works

  • The call. Someone says "Grandma, it's me" or "it's your grandson," often crying or speaking quietly so the voice is hard to judge. Voice-cloning tools mean it can even sound like the real person.
  • The emergency. An arrest, a car accident, a hospital bill or being stuck abroad. There's a reason it has to be dealt with today.
  • The "official." A second caller poses as a lawyer, a police officer or a court clerk, and explains how to pay the "bail" or the "fees."
  • The secret. "Please don't tell Mom and Dad." That one line is what keeps the family out of it, and it's the strongest sign of a scam.
  • The payment. Cash handed to a courier who comes to the door, cash mailed in a parcel, gift cards, a wire transfer or cryptocurrency.

What to do now, in order

  1. Check the grandchild is safe. Call them, or their parents, on a number you already have. Not a number the caller gave.
  2. If a courier is coming to collect cash, call the police. This is the part where police can act quickly, sometimes at the door.
  3. Call your parent's bank. Ask for the fraud team and say "elder financial exploitation." If a transfer or withdrawal is still pending, it may be possible to stop it.
  4. Try to stop what you can. Gift cards: call the company that issued them straight away and keep the cards and receipts. Wire transfers: call the bank or transfer company immediately. Cash sent by mail: call the US Postal Inspection Service on 1-877-876-2455, which can sometimes intercept a parcel.
  5. Report it. File with the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center and with the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. The National Elder Fraud Hotline, 833-372-8311, can help you with both.

What to say to your parent

Many people feel deeply embarrassed after this call, and embarrassment is what makes them hide the next one. Start with the fact that it was a frightening call and that they acted out of love, not foolishness. Then one simple rule:

"No real police officer, lawyer or court will ever send someone to your house to collect cash, ask for gift cards, or tell you to keep it from the family."

Set up a family code word

Agree on a word or question that only the family knows, and that any grandchild or relative in real trouble would be asked for. Keep it out of social media. It turns a panicked decision into a simple check, and it's easy to set up when everyone's together for the holidays.

Expect another call

Once a scam has worked, or nearly worked, the same people often call again: another emergency, a "refund," or someone offering to recover the money for a fee. No real agency charges a fee to get money back.

If your parent is in the UK

The UK version often arrives as a text or WhatsApp message: "Hi Mum, I've lost my phone, this is my new number," followed by a request for money. Call the bank's fraud line, or 159, which connects you safely to most major UK banks. If someone is coming to collect cash, call 999. Report it to Report Fraud (formerly Action Fraud) at reportfraud.police.uk or 0300 123 2040.
