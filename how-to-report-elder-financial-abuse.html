How to report elder financial abuse, and what happens after

You can report it today, without your parent's permission. Here's where to go, what each place actually does, and what not to expect.

The short answer

You can report a parent who is being scammed or financially exploited without asking them first. In the US, the main places are the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center, the FTC, the National Elder Fraud Hotline and Adult Protective Services. Each does something different, and none of them works like calling 911. Report anyway, quickly, and put most of your energy into stopping the next payment.

Before you report: write a short file

Twenty minutes, not twenty hours. Every agency will ask for the same things:

  • Dates and amounts of any payments you know about. Approximate is fine.
  • How the money was sent: bank transfer, card, gift cards, cryptocurrency, cash or check. This decides whether anything can be undone.
  • The name and story the scammer is using, and how they make contact (which app or platform, how often).
  • When it started, and who else knows.
  • For cryptocurrency: wallet addresses, transaction IDs, exact dates and times, and the exchange used.

Use what your parent has told you or what's in plain view. Don't get into their email, phone or accounts without permission. It's a legal risk, and if they find out, it gives them a real grievance against you.

Where to report, and what each one does

  • FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3). Family members can file on someone else's behalf. IC3 collects and routes reports; it won't investigate your case personally or send you updates. But speed matters: the FBI has a rapid-response process that can sometimes freeze money that has only just moved. It works in hours and days, so file immediately.
  • The FTC, at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Reports go into a database used by investigators at federal, state and local level to build cases.
  • National Elder Fraud Hotline: 833-372-8311. Free, staffed on weekdays, and open to calls about someone you know. You're assigned a case manager who helps you report and work out next steps. This is the most hands-on option.
  • Adult Protective Services. Each state runs its own. Family can report and you don't need proof. APS can investigate and offer help, but an adult who still has capacity can refuse it, and many do. If you report, it's better your parent hears it from you first.
  • US Postal Inspection Service: 1-877-876-2455, if cash or checks were sent by mail. They can sometimes intercept a package.
  • Their bank and any brokerage. Not a report to the authorities, but often the most useful call. Investment firms in the US can place a temporary hold on payments out of an older customer's account when they suspect exploitation.

What not to expect

Most money sent to scammers doesn't come back. In 2025 the FBI's recovery team froze roughly three cents for every dollar reported lost, and frozen isn't the same as returned. And most reports become intelligence for investigators rather than cases with a detective assigned to them.

That isn't a reason to skip reporting. It's a reason to measure success differently: not by whether the money comes back, but by whether the next payment happens.

The scam that comes after the report

Once someone has been scammed, they're on a list. Scammers buy and sell these lists, and the next approach is often someone offering to get the money back: a fake law firm that quotes the exact amounts and dates of earlier transfers, or someone pretending to be from the FBI or another agency.

Warn your parent now, before it happens, and frame it as protecting what's left rather than proof they were wrong. The rule to say out loud: no real agency ever asks for a payment to recover your money.

If your parent is in the UK

Report it to Report Fraud, which replaced Action Fraud in December 2025: reportfraud.police.uk or 0300 123 2040 (in Scotland, call Police Scotland on 101). Individual police forces decide whether to investigate, so don't expect a detective to call. If the money went by bank transfer, the more useful route is the bank: since October 2024, banks have to reimburse most scam payments made by standard transfer, up to £85,000. If your parent has care and support needs, you can also make a safeguarding referral to their local council.
