The five calls

Five free phone calls you can make about an ageing parent's money without her permission, and without going through her post, her laptop or her accounts. None of them move her money. None of them need her signature. Most people never learn these exist, and by the time anyone tells them, the conversation has already gone badly.

If you are here because a parent is sending money to someone and will not listen to you — start at call five if any cash went by post, otherwise start at call one. Do the calls before you raise it with her again. The reason why is at the bottom of the page.

Make these in order

1

Her bank — the main current account

Use the phrase that routes you to someone trained for this.

Say exactly this I'd like to speak to someone about an elder financial exploitation concern regarding my mother's account.

Then tell them what you have seen — amounts, where it went, over how long — and that you believe she is being coached by a third party. Say plainly that you are not asking them to discuss her account with you. Then ask:

  • Can you put a note on the account?
  • Do you have a trusted-contact facility?
  • What alerts exist, and what does she have to do to switch them on?

What it cannot doA trusted contact cannot see the account, move money, block a transfer or make any decision. It only gives the bank someone to ring. Knowing that before you call saves you a disappointment.

2

Her brokerage, pension or retirement provider

Most time-sensitive

Ask whether they can place a hold. Investment firms can; banks mostly cannot.

In the US, FINRA Rule 2165 lets a member firm place a temporary hold on a disbursement — and on a securities transaction — when it reasonably believes an account holder aged 65 or over is being financially exploited. The initial hold runs up to fifteen business days. It can be extended by ten more, and by a further thirty if the firm has reported the matter to a state regulator or a court.

Two honest caveatsThe rule is permissive, not mandatory — it says in terms that firms are not required to place a hold. And the first window is short. That combination is what makes this the call to make first among the financial ones.

3

The credit bureaus — one call covers all three

Place a fraud alert. A representative is allowed to do this on her behalf.

Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, section 605A, an initial fraud alert may be requested by the consumer or by someone acting on her behalf, on a good-faith suspicion of fraud. It is one of very few protections the law explicitly lets another person put in place. The alert lasts a year, and the bureau you call is required to pass it to the other nationwide bureaus — which is why one call covers all three.

What it cannot doIt does not freeze anything she already has, and it does not touch her balance. It makes new credit opened quietly in her name harder, and nothing else.

4

National Elder Fraud Hotline

833-372-8311

Free, staffed on weekdays, and explicitly open to people calling about someone else.

Their own wording covers "you or someone you know", so you are allowed to call about her. You are assigned a case manager, which is more than almost any other route gives you.

In the UKAction Fraud, 0300 123 2040

5

US Postal Inspection Service

1-877-876-2455

If any cash went by post, they can sometimes intercept the package.

This only works while the package is still moving, which is usually a matter of days. If cash has gone in an envelope, make this call before you make any of the others.

Two things this page will not do

It will not get the money back. Most of it does not come back, and the published recovery figures are bleak. The point of these five calls is not recovery — it is stopping the next payment.

It will not survive being announced. Alerts and holds work because they are quiet. A person who is being coached is usually also being told what to say to her bank, so the day she is warned is the day most of this stops being available to you. That is the reason to make the calls first and have the conversation second — not to deceive her, but because the order genuinely matters.

The conversation is the hard part

The calls above are the part you can do alone. What they don't solve is the reason you're reading this at all: she doesn't believe you, and every time you raise it, it goes worse than the time before.

Where this comes from

The temporary hold in call two is FINRA Rule 2165, Financial Exploitation of Specified Adults — the rule text and its extension periods are published at finra.org. The fraud alert in call three is section 605A of the Fair Credit Reporting Act (15 U.S.C. §1681c-1), which is where the right of a person acting on the consumer's behalf, the one-year duration and the referral between bureaus all come from. The hotline wording in call four is the hotline's own.

Calls one, two, three and five are US procedures. If you are in the UK, call four's equivalent is Action Fraud and your bank's own vulnerable-customer team is the nearest thing to call one; the rest do not apply.
