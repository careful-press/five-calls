My mom is being scammed and won't listen. What do I do?

You've shown her the evidence. You've explained it calmly, then less calmly. She defended him, and now she's angry with you. Here is what to do next, in the order that actually works.

First: you are not failing to explain it well enough

Almost everyone in your position assumes the problem is information. If she could just see what you see, she'd stop. So you gather screenshots, reverse-image the photos, and present the case.

It doesn't work, and the reason matters. Accepting that it's a scam means accepting two losses at once: that she was fooled, and that the person she's been talking to for months was never there. Researchers who interviewed romance-fraud victims found many rated losing the relationship as worse than losing the money. The harder you push, the more humiliating it becomes for her to climb down. She isn't being stubborn. Admitting it costs her too much, all at once.

And she has very likely been told, in advance, that her family would say exactly this. Isolating the victim from family is part of the script. Your warning was predicted, so it lands as proof that the scammer was right about you.

Step 1: protect the money before you say another word

The most useful things you can do today need nobody's permission and don't touch her money:

  1. Call her bank and ask to speak to someone about an "elder financial exploitation" concern. That phrase routes you to people trained for this. They won't discuss her account with you, but they can note it and watch for the pattern.
  2. If she has a brokerage or retirement account, call that firm too. Under FINRA Rule 2165, brokerage firms can place a temporary hold on disbursements when they reasonably believe a customer aged 65 or over is being exploited.
  3. Place a fraud alert with one credit bureau. The law lets someone acting on her behalf do this, and that bureau must pass it to the other two.
  4. Call the National Elder Fraud Hotline: 833-372-8311. It's free and open to family members calling about someone else.
  5. If any cash was posted, call the Postal Inspection Service right away: 1-877-876-2455. They can sometimes intercept a package still in transit.

Why the order matters: these protections work because they're quiet. Someone who is being coached is usually also being told what to say to her bank. The day she learns you're onto it is the day a lot of these options close. Make the calls first and have the conversation second. You're not deceiving her. The order changes what works.

Step 2: stop arguing about whether it's real

For now, stop trying to win the argument about whether he is real. When she says something you know is false, you don't have to agree. You just don't have to fight it.

She says: "He's had such a hard year."
Not: "He doesn't exist, Mom."
Instead: "That sounds like it's been a lot for you."

You've conceded nothing. You also haven't spent your one conversation on a fight you can't win.

Step 3: aim for one small step, not a confession

The goal of the next conversation is not "she admits it's a scam." It's moving her one small step, from "he's real and everything is fine" to "I could check one small thing." Ask how things work rather than why she believes them: how does the money get to him, why can't he video call, who is holding his documents? The details are where the story is thinnest, and she'll notice the gaps herself sooner than she'll accept you pointing them out.

Step 4: protect your access to her

The best sign a conversation went well isn't whether she agreed. It's whether she still picks up when you call. If you lose access, the scammer has her to himself. After a bad conversation, wait longer before the next one, not shorter. Repeated attempts close together feel like a campaign, and campaigns make people dig in.

What not to do

  • Don't confront her with a pile of evidence in front of other relatives.
  • Don't teach her "the warning signs". In testing, standard scam-spotting tips made people more suspicious of everything without making them more accurate.
  • Don't threaten to take over her money unless you have the legal authority and have spoken to a lawyer. A competent adult's account can't be frozen just because her family asks.

The calls protect the money. The conversation is the hard part.
