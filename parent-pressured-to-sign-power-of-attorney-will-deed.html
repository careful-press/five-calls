Someone is pressuring my parent to sign a power of attorney, a new will or a deed

A new friend, carer or relative is suddenly helping with the paperwork, and your parent is about to sign over control. Here's what undue influence looks like, what you can do before anything is signed, and what to do if it already has been.

The short answer

If papers haven't been signed yet, the most useful step is to make sure your parent sees their own lawyer, alone, before signing anything. If something has already been signed, talk to an elder law attorney in your parent's state soon, and tell their bank and any investment firm that you're worried about who is now controlling the money. If you think your parent is being exploited right now, adult protective services can investigate, and you don't need your parent's permission to make that call.

Signs it may be undue influence

  • A new person at the center of everything. A recent friend, romantic partner, carer or one relative who now handles the calls, the mail and the appointments.
  • Their own lawyer, not your parent's. The new person found the lawyer, drove your parent there and sat in the room.
  • Rushed paperwork. A power of attorney, a new will, a change of beneficiary, adding someone to a bank account, or transferring the house, often all in a short space of time.
  • Isolation. Family calls aren't returned, visits are discouraged, and your parent repeats phrases like "they're the only one who really helps me."
  • Changes that don't sound like your parent. Leaving out children or charities they always cared about, or giving away a house they always said would stay in the family.

Before anything is signed

  1. Ask your parent to see their own lawyer, alone. A good estate planning or elder law attorney will want to meet the client privately and check that the decisions are truly theirs. That's the single strongest protection.
  2. Don't attack the new person. It usually pushes your parent closer to them. Ask questions instead: "What made you decide to change it now? Did you get to talk it through with your own lawyer?"
  3. Raise your concerns with the bank. Tell their bank's fraud team, and any investment firm, that you're worried someone is exploiting your parent. Investment firms can add a trusted contact, and they and many banks can place a temporary hold on a suspicious payment.
  4. Write things down. Dates, who was present, what was said, and any sudden changes in money or behavior. It matters later if you need to involve a lawyer, APS or the courts.

If it has already been signed

  • Talk to an elder law attorney in your parent's state. A power of attorney can be revoked by a parent who has capacity, and in many states a court can review what an agent is doing. A deed or will signed under undue influence can sometimes be challenged.
  • Report suspected exploitation to adult protective services in your parent's county. They can investigate, including whether your parent understood what they signed.
  • Protect the house. Many county recorders offer a free property fraud alert that emails you when a document is filed against an address. Check with the county recorder or clerk where the property is.
  • Guardianship is the last resort, not the first. It removes your parent's rights, takes time and money, and can damage the relationship for good. A lawyer can tell you whether a less drastic option will do.

What to say to your parent

Your parent may feel that questioning the new person means questioning their judgment. Keep the focus on their independence, not on who's right:

"It's your money and your decision. I just want you to talk it through with your own lawyer, on your own, before you sign anything."

If your parent is in the UK

A Lasting Power of Attorney has to be registered with the Office of the Public Guardian (OPG), and family members can object during registration. If you're worried an attorney is misusing an LPA that's already registered, you can report it to the OPG, which can investigate. You can also raise a safeguarding concern with your parent's local council, and HM Land Registry offers a free Property Alert service for the house.
