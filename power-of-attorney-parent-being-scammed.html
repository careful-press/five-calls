Can I get power of attorney if my parent is being scammed?

It's the first thing most families reach for, and the most common misunderstanding. Here's what a power of attorney can and can't do, and what helps in the meantime.

The short answer

Only if your parent agrees to sign one. And even then, it won't let you stop them. A power of attorney is a document your parent signs to give you authority to act for them. They have to understand what they're signing and choose to sign it. While they can still make their own decisions, they keep full control of their money and can cancel the power of attorney whenever they like.

So if your mom is sending money to a scammer and refuses to believe it, a power of attorney won't let you block the payments, close her accounts or overrule her. It's a tool for when someone can't act for themselves. It isn't a way to override them while they can.

What a power of attorney actually does

  • It adds you, it doesn't replace her. You can act alongside your parent on the things the document covers. She can still act herself, including doing exactly what she's doing now.
  • She can cancel it. As long as she has capacity, she can revoke your authority at any time. If a scammer is coaching her, revoking it may be one of the first things they push her to do.
  • Some only start later. Depending on the state, a power of attorney can be written to take effect only if a doctor certifies she can no longer manage her affairs. Until then it does nothing.
  • Banks check it, and may still say no. Many institutions review the document before accepting it, and some ask for their own forms. Getting one signed is not the same as being able to use it the same day.

Why asking for one right now can backfire

Scammers often warn their targets that family will try to take control of their money. If the first thing you do after finding out is ask your parent to sign over authority, it can sound like proof that the scammer was right. Many families find their parent digs in harder after that conversation.

If you want a power of attorney in place, and it's a good idea for everyone over 60, raise it as ordinary planning, separate from the scam, ideally with a neutral person such as their own lawyer. Don't make it the price of an argument you're trying to win.

What you can do this week without one

  • Call their bank's fraud team. They won't discuss the account with you, but they can listen, note it and watch it. Ask for whoever handles elder financial exploitation.
  • Call any brokerage or retirement account firm. US investment firms can place a temporary hold on a payment out of an older customer's account when they suspect exploitation, for around 15 business days, with extensions possible. They may do this; they don't have to.
  • Ask your parent to add a trusted contact on their investment accounts. It's a person the firm may contact if it's worried. It gives you no authority over the account, which is exactly why parents are more willing to agree to it.
  • Help them freeze their credit. A credit freeze is free and stops new accounts being opened in their name. Your parent places it with each of the three bureaus, but you can sit with them while they do it.
  • Report it. The National Elder Fraud Hotline (833-372-8311) can help you report and work out next steps. Adult Protective Services takes reports from family, and you don't need proof. Be aware that an adult who has capacity can refuse APS help.

When guardianship comes into it

If your parent truly can no longer understand their own decisions, and didn't sign a power of attorney while they could, the route is a court: guardianship or conservatorship. A judge takes authority over their affairs away from them and gives it to someone else.

This is meant to be a last resort, and for good reason. It's slow and expensive, mostly because of attorney fees, and it removes rights that are rarely restored. A federal report found guardianship is often imposed when it isn't warranted. The key question is capacity, not whether the decision is a good one. Making a bad choice with your money isn't, on its own, proof that you can't decide.

There's also a separate idea called undue influence: a person can have full capacity and still be manipulated into acting against their own interests. That often describes a scam victim better than "she's lost capacity," and it's something an elder law attorney in your parent's state can advise on. The legal tests vary by state.

If your parent is in the UK

A Lasting Power of Attorney has to be made while your parent still has mental capacity, and registered before it can be used. The law is explicit that someone isn't treated as lacking capacity just because they make an unwise decision. If capacity is lost and there's no LPA, the route is deputyship through the Court of Protection. There's also better news on the money itself: since October 2024, banks have to reimburse most scam payments made by standard bank transfer, up to £85,000, so report it to the bank quickly.
