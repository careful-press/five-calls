My parent is in a romance scam and won't believe me

He's never video-called. There's always another emergency. You can see it. Your mom (or dad) can't, or won't, and every time you say so they move closer to him.

Why they defend him

Romance scams follow a known sequence: a profile built to fit your parent, an intense relationship that feels unusually deep, unusually fast, then a small first request, then a crisis, then more crises until the money runs out. These relationships often run for months, sometimes years.

By the time you notice, your parent has something real at stake: feelings that were real to them even though he isn't. Asking them to accept it's a scam is asking them to grieve and to feel foolish on the same afternoon. Many victims describe losing the relationship as worse than losing the money. That's why the clearer and more undeniable you make your case, the harder they hold on.

He told them you'd say this

Secrecy is part of the script, framed as protecting the relationship: your family won't understand, they'll try to stop us. So when you say "this is a scam," you're doing exactly what he predicted. It helps to say this to yourself before you say anything to them: they're not choosing him over you. They were set up, months ago, so your warning would sound like jealousy.

The first conversation should be about them, not about him

Don't attack the relationship. Ask about it. "What's he like?" is a better opening than anything you've been rehearsing. Then ask about the mechanics, gently and with real curiosity:

  • How does the money actually get to him?
  • Why can't he video call? What happened the last time you tried?
  • Who is holding his documents or his inheritance, and why can't they release it?

The logistics are where the story is thinnest. A question they have to answer out loud does more than a fact you hand them.

Watch for the money turning into an "investment"

If he starts talking about a trading platform, an account showing returns on a screen, or crypto, that's the same scam further along. The biggest losses among older adults often start as relationships and only later become "investments". Some operations let one small withdrawal go through on purpose, so the next deposit is bigger. If your parent has a brokerage or retirement account, call that firm now. Under FINRA Rule 2165 they can place a temporary hold on withdrawals when they suspect exploitation of a customer aged 65 or older.

Things not to say

  • "He's not real." You may be certain. Said in the first conversation, it spends everything for nothing.
  • "How could you be so stupid?" Shame is what's keeping them in it.
  • "If you send him one more dollar, I'm calling the police / taking over your money." Threats feel like control, and anger at being controlled tends to come with counter-arguments. Then you're the one being defended against.

Do the quiet things first

Before you raise it again, call their bank and ask for someone who handles elder financial exploitation, place a fraud alert with a credit bureau, and call the National Elder Fraud Hotline (833-372-8311). The five calls are explained here, free. They don't need your parent's permission, and they work best before the scammer learns you know.
