Seeing your parents for the holidays? Signs they're being scammed, and what to do

Thanksgiving and Christmas are often the first time in months you see your parents' everyday life up close. Here's what to look for, what not to do at the table, and what to do before you leave.

The short answer

Look, don't confront. If you notice signs that a parent is being scammed, the first move is a quiet call to their bank's fraud team, not a conversation over dinner. Challenging a parent in front of the family is the fastest way to make them defend the scammer, and many scammers prepare their targets for exactly that moment.

Signs to look for

  • Someone new they talk to a lot but have never met. Often someone they met on Facebook, a game app or a dating site. Watch for constant texting, or a story about a friend overseas, in the military or on an oil rig.
  • Gift cards, crypto or wire transfers they can't explain. Stacks of gift cards, receipts from a cryptocurrency ATM, money transfer slips, or questions about how to buy any of these.
  • Money that's suddenly tight. Unpaid bills, overdraft letters or a request to borrow money, while they insist nothing has changed.
  • Secrecy about the phone, computer or bank. A parent who used to be open and now says it's private, or whose story keeps changing.
  • Strange software or warnings on the computer. A remote-access app they can't explain, or pop-ups telling them to call a number.
  • Prizes and "opportunities." Letters or calls about lotteries, sweepstakes or investments that only need a fee or a transfer first.
  • Being told to keep it from family. This is the biggest red flag of all. A real bank, agency or partner doesn't need your parent to hide anything from you.

What not to do at the table

  • Don't announce "you're being scammed." In front of others it sounds like an accusation, and your parent will defend themselves by defending the scammer.
  • Don't lay out the evidence. Arguments with proof rarely work in the moment. They tend to end with your parent more committed, not less.
  • Don't take the phone or change passwords behind their back. It's a legal and practical risk, and it confirms what the scammer has probably told them: that family wants to control their money.

What to do before you leave

  1. Call their bank's fraud team. Ask for whoever handles elder financial exploitation and tell them what you've seen. They won't discuss the account with you, but they can watch it and question unusual payments.
  2. Ask about a trusted contact. If your parent has a brokerage or retirement account, ask them to name you as a trusted contact. It gives you no control over the account, which is exactly why many parents agree. In the US, investment firms can also place a temporary hold on a payment when they suspect exploitation.
  3. Offer to help with the computer. With their permission, sit with them while you check for remote-access apps and run updates. Make it about keeping the computer working, not about catching them out.
  4. Freeze their credit together. It's free, it stops new accounts being opened in their name, and your parent can do it with each of the three bureaus while you sit with them.
  5. Save one number. The National Elder Fraud Hotline, 833-372-8311, helps family members work out what to do next and assigns a case manager.

One sentence worth saying

If there's a calm moment, one plain rule does more than any argument:

"No real bank, police force or government department will ever ask you to move money to keep it safe, pay with gift cards, or keep it secret from your family."

It's simply true, it doesn't accuse anyone, and it's the line most likely to come back to your parent the next time the phone rings.

If your parent is in the UK

Call the bank's fraud line, or 159, which connects you safely to most major UK banks. Since October 2024, banks have to reimburse most scam payments made by standard bank transfer, up to £85,000, so speed matters. Report it to Report Fraud (formerly Action Fraud) at reportfraud.police.uk or 0300 123 2040.
