A caller told my parent they're from Social Security or the IRS

A threat of arrest, a suspended Social Security number or a tax debt that has to be paid today. Here's how government impersonation scams work, what the real agencies will never do, and what to do if your parent already paid.

The short answer

Have your parent hang up. Real government agencies don't call out of the blue threatening arrest, and they never demand payment by gift card, wire transfer, cryptocurrency or cash. If your parent wants to check, they can call Social Security or the IRS on the number from the agency's official website or a letter they already have, never the number the caller gave. If money or personal details have already gone, call their bank today and report it.

What the calls sound like

  • "Your Social Security number has been suspended." Often linked to a made-up crime, like a car found with drugs in it registered to their number.
  • "You owe back taxes and a warrant will be issued today." Sometimes with a fake badge number or a second caller posing as the police.
  • "Your benefits will stop unless you verify your details." A way to collect their Social Security number, bank details or Medicare number.
  • "Move your money to protect it." They're told their accounts are compromised and to transfer savings, withdraw cash or buy gold "for safekeeping."
  • Official-looking proof. Caller ID showing a real agency number, a text with a seal, or a photo of an "ID badge" or "court document." All of these can be faked.

What the real agencies will never do

  • Social Security won't threaten arrest or legal action because your parent doesn't pay immediately, and won't suspend their Social Security number.
  • Neither Social Security nor the IRS will demand payment by gift card, prepaid card, wire transfer, cryptocurrency or cash sent by mail or courier.
  • The IRS normally contacts people about a tax debt by letter first, and taxpayers have the right to question or appeal what they owe.
  • No real agency will tell your parent to keep the call secret from their family or their bank.

If your parent already paid or gave out details

  1. Call their bank's fraud team today. Say "elder financial exploitation" and ask whether any transfers are still pending.
  2. If they used gift cards, call the gift card company straight away using the number on the back of the card and ask if the money can be frozen.
  3. If they gave their Social Security number, freeze their credit with all three credit bureaus (it's free) and consider an IRS Identity Protection PIN, which stops anyone else filing a tax return in their name.
  4. Report it. Social Security impersonation goes to the Social Security Office of the Inspector General at oig.ssa.gov. IRS impersonation goes to the Treasury Inspector General for Tax Administration at tigta.gov. Report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov as well.
  5. Get help if you need it. The National Elder Fraud Hotline, 833-372-8311, can walk you through the reports.

The rule to give your parent

These calls work by fear and speed. A rule your parent can use while still on the phone takes both away:

"No government agency will ever arrest you over the phone or ask for gift cards or cash. Hang up, and call me before you do anything."

Expect another call

Once a parent has paid, the same people often call back as a different "agency" or as a lawyer offering to recover the money for a fee. No real agency charges a fee to get money back.

If your parent is in the UK

The same scam comes as calls or texts from "HMRC" or "the DWP" about tax owed or benefits being stopped. HMRC and the DWP won't threaten arrest or ask for payment by gift card or crypto. Forward scam texts to 7726, call the bank on 159 if money has moved, and report it to Report Fraud (formerly Action Fraud) at reportfraud.police.uk or 0300 123 2040.
