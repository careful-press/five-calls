How to stop an elderly parent sending money to a scammer

The honest answer first: if your parent is a competent adult, nobody can freeze their money just because the family asks. But you can do far more than most families realise, and a lot of it doesn't need their permission.

What you can do without their permission

  1. Their bank. Ask for someone who handles an "elder financial exploitation" concern. Describe what you've seen: amounts, where it went, over how long. They can't discuss the account with you, but they can note it and watch it. Ask whether they offer a trusted contact. That's someone the bank may contact if it suspects exploitation. A trusted contact can't see or move money; it's an early-warning line.
  2. Their brokerage or retirement account. Under FINRA Rule 2165, brokerage firms may place a temporary hold on disbursements when they reasonably believe a customer aged 65 or older is being financially exploited. Banks generally don't have an equivalent federal rule, though some states have their own hold laws. If there's an investment account, this is the call to make first.
  3. A fraud alert. You can place one with a single credit bureau on their behalf. That bureau must notify the other two. It makes it harder for anyone to open new credit in their name.
  4. The National Elder Fraud Hotline: 833-372-8311. Free, run for the Department of Justice, and open to family members calling about someone else.
  5. Cash in the post: call the Postal Inspection Service immediately on 1-877-876-2455. They can sometimes intercept a package that's still moving.

Make these calls before you confront your parent again. If they're being coached, they're usually being told what to say to their bank too.

What needs more than a phone call

Power of attorney. A durable power of attorney lets someone act on another person's finances, but only if your parent signs it while they still have capacity. If they're refusing to accept there's a scam, they may well refuse to sign. Talk to an elder law attorney about what's realistic in your state. Note too that having a POA doesn't give you the right to override decisions your parent is still able to make.

Adult Protective Services. Every US state has an APS agency that investigates financial exploitation of vulnerable adults. A report can lead to an investigation, but APS generally can't force a competent adult to accept help. It matters most where there are signs of cognitive decline.

Guardianship or conservatorship. This is a court process that removes some of a person's rights. It is slow, expensive and hard on relationships, and it's only available if a court finds your parent lacks capacity. It's a last resort, not a first move.

The line that changes everything: capacity

A parent who understands the transaction and disagrees with you is in a completely different legal position from one who can't understand it. If you're seeing memory problems, confusion about money they used to handle easily, or new difficulty following conversations, mention it to their doctor and to an elder law attorney. That changes what's available to you.

What won't work

  • Asking the bank to "just block it". Without legal authority, they won't.
  • Taking their cards or phone without consent. It usually destroys trust, and they'll find another way to pay.
  • Expecting the money back. Most of it isn't recovered. The realistic goal is stopping the next payment.

The calls stop the money. They don't stop the relationship.
