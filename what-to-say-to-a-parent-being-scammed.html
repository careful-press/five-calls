What to say to a parent who's being scammed

Most families try the same thing: lay out the evidence, clearly and calmly. It almost never works, and it can push your parent closer to the scammer. Here's what to say instead.

Why the obvious approach backfires

Treating the conversation as a matter of delivering the truth assumes your parent is short of information. Usually they aren't. They're short of a way to accept it that doesn't cost them everything at once: their money, their self-respect, and often a relationship that felt real. The more undeniable you make it, the more humiliating it is to agree.

Counsellors have a name for the urge you'll feel about ninety seconds in: the righting reflex, the need to correct something obviously untrue. Professional guidance is to hold it back, because the more someone is confronted, the more they argue for the thing you want them to drop.

Five things to say

  1. "Tell me about him. What's he like?" Open with them, not the scam. Every minute they're talking is a minute they're not defending.
  2. "How does the money get to him?" Ask how it works, not why they believe it. Explaining the mechanics out loud is often where people notice the gaps themselves.
  3. "That sounds really hard." When they say something false ("he's had a terrible year"), you don't have to agree. You just don't have to fight it.
  4. "Would you be willing to check one small thing?" Not "admit it's a scam." One thing: a video call, a reverse image search done together, a call to the bank from their own phone.
  5. "I'm not going to keep bringing this up. If anything changes, I'd rather know than not know." This keeps the door open, and an open door is worth more than a won argument.

Five things never to say

  1. "He's not real." Even if you're sure. In a first conversation it spends everything for nothing.
  2. "How could you fall for this?" Shame is what's keeping them in it.
  3. "Everyone in the family knows." Now admitting it means facing all of you.
  4. "Here are the warning signs." In testing, scam-spotting tips made people more suspicious of everything, real messages included, without making them more accurate.
  5. "Do this or I'll take over your money." Threats feel like control, and people defend their right to decide for themselves, even when they're deciding badly.

How to tell if it went well

After each conversation, ask yourself four questions, and write the answers down:

  • Did they tell me anything new?
  • Are they more or less likely to pick up when I call?
  • Did they defend him more strongly, or less?
  • Did I get a yes to anything, however small?

If two or more went the wrong way, wait longer before the next attempt. Going back quickly to "fix it" feels right and usually makes it worse.
